Professor Barber,

I built a working AI system where many computers each run their own complete AI model, all working on different parts of the same task simultaneously, in true parallel, with zero communication between them. A coordinator breaks the task, distributes pieces, and combines results. No cloud, no external dependency, no data leaving the local network. It runs today, free, open source, every line inspectable.

I think this is directly relevant to what you are building with the UK sovereign AI initiative — and I would genuinely value your evaluation, because I think it solves a problem that the current approach may not have addressed yet: resilience.

Your ambition is right. Buy NVIDIA GPUs. Build sovereign AI capability for Britain. Make Britain an AI superpower. But consider WHERE those GPUs go. A single sovereign AI centre — no matter how powerful — is a single point of failure. Sensitive to infrastructure disruption, to the kind of threats Britain faces, to technological shifts that could make today's hardware investment obsolete before it is finished.

Britain learned this lesson before. In WW2, British industry survived the Blitz because factories were dispersed across the country, not concentrated in London. And Britain won the Battle of Britain not with one giant radar installation but with Chain Home — a network of independent radar stations distributed along the coast. Each station operated independently. If the Luftwaffe bombed one, the others still detected incoming aircraft. The system was resilient because it was distributed.

What I am suggesting is Chain Home for AI.

Instead of concentrating all sovereign compute in one centre, distribute powerful GPU nodes across Britain's most strategically critical institutions — major hospitals, army bases, intelligence facilities, key universities. Each location becomes a powerful coordinator node in its own right, running my system locally, keeping its own data entirely within its own walls. For 95% of tasks, any single node with its local workers is more than enough.

And if the worst happens — if a node is hit, if infrastructure fails — the others compensate. Two institutions can share a coordinator. The network adapts. No single point of failure can bring down British AI capability, because there is no single point.

The architecture scales through hierarchy: each powerful node can coordinate lower-level nodes beneath it, which coordinate their own workers. Intelligence cascades through layers. If any level is disrupted, the hierarchy reorganises. This is described in detail in the epilogue of my book "The Distributed AI Revolution" on GitHub.

For the rare 5% of tasks that require more compute than any single node has, coordinator nodes at the same level can slowly synchronise work between them — not as fast as one giant supercomputer, but far more resilient, and available even in a scenario where American and Chinese cloud AI is no longer accessible to Britain.

This is not the shiny diamond that impresses at conferences. This is the hard diamond that survives when everything else falls apart. Britain has always won not by being the biggest, but by being the smartest about how it distributes its strength. The navy across the seven seas. Chain Home along the coast. Greenwich synchronising the world. This is the same pattern, applied to AI.

I don't have formal credentials in your field, and I might be wrong about how significant this is. But you are building sovereign AI for Britain, and I think resilience deserves a place in that conversation. Would you take a look?
