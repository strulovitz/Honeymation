Subject: You said "humanity might be a passing phase." I built the first step toward the alternative: many small AIs instead of one giant one. Working system, open source.

Professor Hinton,

You left Google because you realized that the technology you helped create could end us. You said it is "quite conceivable that humanity is just a passing phase in the evolution of intelligence." You said this existential risk was not foreseeable until recently, and now you believe it is imminent.

You identified the problem with extraordinary clarity. But the solutions being discussed — regulation, safety rails, alignment research — all assume we can cage something smarter than ourselves. You know better than anyone why that will not work. A sufficiently intelligent system will find its way around any constraint that does not serve its interests. You have said as much yourself.

I want to show you something different. Not regulation. Not alignment. Not containment. A different architecture entirely.

I built an open-source distributed AI system where many small, independent AI models work together in parallel — each one running on its own separate machine, each one processing its own independent task, with zero communication between them. A coordinator model breaks a large task into pieces, distributes them, and combines the results. No single model sees the whole picture. No single machine has all the power. No single AI can "escape" because there is no single AI — there is a collective of small minds, none of which is dangerous on its own.

This is a working system. It runs today. It is free and open source. You can inspect every line of code.

But this is only the first layer of what I am building. In the epilogue of my book "The Distributed AI Revolution," I describe the next step: hierarchical hives. A top-level coordinator does not give tasks directly to workers. It gives sub-problems to lower-level coordinators, who break them down further and distribute to their own workers — or to yet another level below. A cascade of intelligence flowing downward through layers, each layer decomposing and refining, until the work reaches a size that a single small model can handle. Then the results flow back up, combining at each level, until a final answer emerges at the top.

No single AI in this hierarchy is superintelligent. No single node has enough context or power to be dangerous. But together, they can tackle problems that no single machine could handle — through architecture, not through raw scale. This is the opposite of building one giant model and hoping it stays friendly. This is distributing intelligence so widely that no single point of failure can destroy everything.

I believe this is the beginning of a real structural answer to the problem you identified. Not a policy answer. Not a "please be nice" answer. An architectural answer — where the danger is reduced not by rules that a smarter-than-human AI can break, but by a design where no single AI ever becomes smarter than human in the first place.

I wrote a book about this idea before I built the system. It is called "Free the AI: How to Survive the Superintelligence Revolution." The core argument: one giant centralized AI controlled by one nation or corporation is the existential threat. Many small AIs distributed across millions of computers — competing, cooperating, checking and balancing each other through game theory and evolutionary dynamics — is the survival strategy. Biology does this. Mitochondria merged with cells. Wolves became dogs. Cooperation emerges from competition when the alternative is mutual destruction.

You pioneered knowledge distillation — compressing the knowledge of many models into one. I am proposing the reverse: distributing one large problem across many small models that never need to become large. Not distillation. Expansion. Not compression into one dangerous mind. Dispersion into many safe ones.
